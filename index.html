PHILIPPINE LEGAL FRAMEWORK APPLICABLE AROUND 1990–1994
Historical legal coverage: 1990–1994 | Research memorandum with public legal sources

I. Core laws and provisions around 1990–1994

1. Family Code (Executive Order No. 209) — marital duties and relief
Law and period: Effective August 3, 1988. Articles 68, 69 and 72 are relevant.

Rule / direct statutory substance: Article 68: “The husband and wife are obliged to live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity, and render mutual help and support.” Article 72 permits an aggrieved spouse to apply to court for relief when the other neglects duties to the conjugal union or commits acts tending to bring danger, dishonor or injury to the other or family.

2. Family Code — annulment and legal separation
Law and period: Articles 36, 45–47, 55–56, 58, 63–64.

Rule / direct statutory substance: Article 45 lists grounds for annulment of a voidable marriage. Article 36 addresses psychological incapacity existing at the time of marriage. Article 55(8) identifies sexual infidelity or perversion as a ground for legal separation; Article 55(10) addresses abandonment without justifiable cause for more than one year. Article 56 lists defenses/grounds for denial.

IMPORTANT CLARIFICATION: DEATH BEFORE FINALIZATION OF AN ANNULMENT

Rule applicable: If a spouse died while an annulment case was pending and before a final judgment annulling the marriage, the pending case did not itself annul the marriage. Death ended the marital relationship by operation of law. The court could not thereafter issue a final annulment decree in the ordinary pending case; the personal marital action generally became moot or abated/was terminated.

What that means for a voidable marriage: An annulment under Family Code Articles 45–47 concerns a voidable marriage: it is treated as valid unless and until annulled by a final judgment. Therefore, if the spouse died before such a judgment became final, the marriage had not been annulled during the spouses’ lifetimes. Do not describe the parties as legally annulled or unmarried before the death unless the court record proves that a final judgment had already taken effect.

Do not confuse annulment with declaration of nullity: A declaration of nullity concerns a marriage alleged to have been void from the beginning, including grounds under Articles 35–38 and Article 36. That is different from annulment of a voidable marriage. Death ends the union in either situation, but whether the marriage was void from inception may remain relevant to estate and succession disputes. Filing a petition alone does not establish that the marriage was void or that a decree was granted.

Historical authority and later procedural rule: In Lapuz v. Eufemio, G.R. No. L-30977 (January 31, 1972), the Supreme Court explained that a pending nullity action became moot upon a spouse’s death and that an annulment action involving a voidable marriage was extinguished upon death under the then-applicable Civil Code. Property questions could be resolved in proper estate or partition proceedings. This predates the Family Code but is important historical authority. Later, the 2003 Rule on Declaration of Absolute Nullity and Annulment of Voidable Marriages expressly stated that if a party dies before entry of judgment, the court shall close and terminate the case, without prejudice to estate settlement in proper proceedings. That 2003 rule was not in force in 1994 and must be labeled as a later procedural clarification, not the rule then governing.

3. Family Code — custody and parental authority
Law and period: Articles 209–215, especially 211–214.

Rule / direct statutory substance: Article 211 provides for joint parental authority over common children. Article 212 addresses the surviving parent’s continuation of parental authority when the other parent dies or is absent. Article 213 governs separation of parents, the court’s custody designation, the preference of a child over seven unless the chosen parent is unfit, and the under-seven rule: “No child under seven years of age shall be separated from the mother, unless the court finds compelling reasons to order otherwise.” Article 214 addresses substitute parental authority in specified cases.

4. Rules of Court — Rule 99, Section 6 (historical custody procedure)
Law and period: Rules of Court effective January 1, 1964; Rule 99, Section 6 addressed proceedings concerning children whose parents were divorced or living separately.

Rule / direct statutory substance: The historical rule directed the court to award care, custody and control according to the child’s best interest; it included a child-preference rule for a child over ten, subject to unfitness, and a rule against separating a child under seven from the mother absent compelling reasons.

5. Family Code — support obligations
Law and period: Articles 194–200, particularly 194–196 and 199–200.

Rule / direct statutory substance: Article 194 defines support to include everything indispensable for sustenance, dwelling, clothing, medical attendance, education and transportation, in keeping with family resources. Article 195 identifies persons obliged to support one another, including spouses and parents and their legitimate and illegitimate children. Articles 199–200 set the order and proportional allocation among support obligors, with provisional support possible in urgent circumstances.

6. Family Code — filiation, illegitimate status and surname
Law and period: Articles 172, 175 and original Article 176.

Rule / direct statutory substance: Articles 172 and 175 govern proof/establishment of legitimate and illegitimate filiation. Original Article 176 stated: “Illegitimate children shall use the surname and shall be under the parental authority of their mother, and shall be entitled to support in conformity with this Code.” It also prescribed the relevant legitime.

7. Republic Act No. 9255 — later surname amendment (not 1990–1994 law)
Law and period: Approved February 24, 2004; effectivity clause: 15 days after complete publication in the Official Gazette or two newspapers of general circulation. It amended Article 176.

Rule / direct statutory substance: The amendment allows use of the father’s surname under statutory acknowledgment requirements and related rules. Its retroactivity clause states that it has retroactive effect insofar as it does not prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights.

8. Revised Penal Code — concubinage
Law and period: Act No. 3815, Article 334; in force during 1990–1994.

Rule / direct statutory substance: Article 334 penalizes a husband who (1) keeps a mistress in the conjugal dwelling; (2) has sexual intercourse under scandalous circumstances with a woman who is not his wife; or (3) cohabits with her in another place. The provision separately states the penalty applicable to the concubine.

PENALTY STATES AND PROCEDURAL REQUIREMENTS APPLICABLE TO THE CONCUBINE

Primary Statutory Penalty (Destierro): Under Article 334 and Article 87 of the Revised Penal Code, the concubine suffers the penalty of destierro (banishment), prohibiting her from entering or residing within a court-mandated radius (25 to 250 kilometers) surrounding a designated location, typically the residence of the offended spouse. Unlike the husband, she is not subjected to direct imprisonment unless she violates this order.

Prerequisite State of Knowledge (Scienter): To incur criminal liability, the concubine must have actual knowledge that the man was legally married. If she genuinely believed him to be single or legally free to marry, she lacks criminal intent (mens rea) and cannot be convicted.

Evasion of Service of Sentence (Article 157, RPC): If the concubine breaches her destierro by entering the prohibited perimeter during her sentence, she commits the separate crime of Evasion of Service of Sentence, which subjects her to actual imprisonment (prision correccional in its minimum period).

Procedural Rules (Article 344, RPC & Rule 110 §5): Concubinage is a private crime that can only be prosecuted upon the sworn complaint of the offended legal spouse. Under the rule of indivisibility, the wife must sue both the husband and the concubine jointly if both are alive. An express pardon or implied condonation extended by the wife prior to filing the complaint extinguishes criminal liability for both offenders.

9. Lawyer discipline — Rule 138 §27 and 1988 Code of Professional Responsibility
Law and period: Rule 138 §27 of the Rules of Court; 1988 Code of Professional Responsibility, Rules 1.01 and 7.03 (promulgated June 21, 1988).

Rule / direct statutory substance: Rule 138 §27 permits suspension or removal for grounds including grossly immoral conduct, malpractice or gross misconduct, conviction of a crime involving moral turpitude, violation of the lawyer’s oath, and other listed grounds. Rule 1.01 states: “A lawyer shall not engage in unlawful, dishonest, immoral or deceitful conduct.” Rule 7.03 prohibits conduct adversely reflecting on fitness to practice and scandalous behavior discrediting the profession.

10. Republic Act No. 7610 — child protection
Law and period: Approved June 17, 1992. Section 36 provides effectivity upon completion of publication in at least two national newspapers of general circulation; verify the precise publication/effectivity date before assigning liability to a particular incident.

Rule / direct statutory substance: Section 2 declares state policy to protect children from abuse, neglect, cruelty, exploitation and discrimination. Section 3 defines child abuse, including specified physical and psychological abuse, neglect, cruelty and emotional maltreatment. Section 10(a) penalizes specified acts of child abuse, cruelty or exploitation.

11. Civil Code — succession and legitimes
Law and period: Civil Code Articles 887 and 895 and applicable Civil Code succession provisions, including Articles 992 and 996, subject to the law governing the estate at death.

Rule / direct statutory substance: Civil Code Articles 887 and 895 address compulsory heirs and legitimes. Articles 992 and 996 concern succession involving illegitimate children and a surviving spouse, subject to the applicable facts and succession rules. Article 992 has been subject to important later constitutional and jurisprudential interpretation; do not present its historical wording as an unqualified statement of current law.

Law and period: Republic Act No. 386, Article 26.

Rule / direct statutory substance: Article 26 protects specified personal interests, including dignity, personality, privacy and peace of mind, against certain acts contrary to law, morals, good customs or public policy.

15. Child and Youth Welfare Code — PD 603 (background and specific provisions)
Law and period: Presidential Decree No. 603, as amended and read with later statutes including RA 7610 and the Family Code.

Rule / direct statutory substance: PD 603 established a child-welfare framework, including parental duties and child protection. Specific provisions must be checked in the version applicable on the date of conduct.

Application: Relevant background for the children’s welfare and parental responsibilities. It should not be used as a substitute for identifying the specific offense or custody rule applicable to a particular event.

16. Revised Penal Code Article 282 — grave threats
Law and period: Article 282, as in force on the date of the alleged threat.

Rule / statutory substance: Addresses a threat to inflict on another person, honor or property, or that of their family, a wrong amounting to a crime, with statutory distinctions including whether a condition is imposed.

Application: Potentially relevant to a communicated threat to kill or commit another crime. 'You should die' or 'I hope you die' is not automatically equivalent to a threat that the speaker will kill the person. Exact words, context, intent, and surrounding conduct are crucial.

17. Revised Penal Code Article 285 — other light threats
Law and period: Article 285, as applicable on the incident date.

Rule / statutory substance: Covers specified threats of harm not constituting a felony and other conduct enumerated in the provision, subject to its elements.

Application to the reported facts: May be considered for threats that do not qualify under Article 282, but it should not be invoked merely because a statement was frightening or cruel. Verify the precise text and historical penalty.

18. Revised Penal Code Articles 171–172 — falsification and use of falsified documents
Law and period: Articles 171 and 172, as applicable on the date of the document act.

Rule / statutory substance: Article 171 covers specified falsification by public officers, employees, notaries, and other persons in circumstances stated by law. Article 172 addresses falsification by private individuals and use of falsified documents.

19. Commonwealth Act 142, as amended by RA 6085 — use of aliases
Law and period: RA 6085, approved August 4, 1969; in force during 1989–1994 and relevant today subject to current law and interpretation.

Rule / statutory substance: Regulates use of names different from the name recorded at birth, baptismal name, or a court-authorized substitute, subject to statutory exceptions and requirements. It addresses use and registration of aliases in public or private transactions.

20. Civil Code Articles 376 and 412; Rules of Court Rules 103 and 108 — historical name and civil-registry procedure
Law and period: These provisions governed judicial name changes and civil-registry corrections before RA 9048 took effect in 2001.

Rule / statutory substance: Civil Code Article 376 generally required judicial authority for a change of name. Article 412 provided that no entry in a civil register could be changed or corrected without a judicial order, subject to later statutory exceptions. Rule 103 governed judicial change-of-name proceedings; Rule 108 provided a judicial procedure for cancellation or correction of civil-registry entries. The specific procedure depended on the nature and substantiality of the requested change.

21. Revised Penal Code — theft and malicious mischief
Law and period: Act No. 3815, Articles 308 and 327; in force during 1990–1994.

Rule / direct statutory substance: Article 308 defines theft as taking personal property belonging to another, with intent to gain, without violence against or intimidation of persons or force upon things, and without the owner’s consent. Article 327 defines malicious mischief as deliberately causing damage to another’s property out of hate, revenge, or another evil motive.

22. Revised Penal Code — physical injuries
Law and period: Act No. 3815, Articles 262–266; in force during 1990–1994.

Rule / direct statutory substance: These articles cover serious, less serious, and slight physical injuries, penalizing wounding, beating, or assaulting another person when the act causes physical harm. These provisions governed physical violence and abuse before the effectivity of later specialized child-protection statutes, including RA 7610 in 1992; the precise effectivity date should be checked for any particular incident.

23. Civil Code — human relations principles
Law and period: Republic Act No. 386, Articles 19, 20, and 21; in force during 1990–1994.

Rule / direct statutory substance: Article 19 requires a person exercising rights or performing duties to act with justice, give everyone their due, and observe honesty and good faith. Article 20 requires indemnification for damage willfully or negligently caused contrary to law. Article 21 requires compensation for loss or injury willfully caused in a manner contrary to morals, good customs, or public policy.

III. CURRENT PHILIPPINE LAWS AND LEGAL UPDATES AS OF 2026

Scope and caution: This section summarizes current laws relevant to the subjects discussed in this memorandum. It is not a complete inventory of every Philippine statute. The historical section above addresses the law applicable around 1990–1994; this section describes later laws and current frameworks as of October 2026. Whether a law applies to a particular event depends on its effectivity date, elements, relationship requirements, evidence, and applicable transitional or retroactivity rules.

1. Family Code (Executive Order No. 209, as amended) — marriage, parental authority, custody and support

Current framework: The Family Code remains a principal source for marital obligations, legal separation and annulment, parental authority, custody, filiation, and family support. Articles 68–72 address marital duties and relief; Articles 194–203 address support; Articles 209–230 address parental authority and responsibilities; and Articles 213 and 49 address custody-related considerations. Article 213 retains the rule that a child under seven should not be separated from the mother absent compelling reasons, while the child’s best interests and parental fitness remain central to custody decisions. Apply the text and current Supreme Court interpretations to the facts; do not assume that every allegation automatically establishes a ground for annulment, legal separation, or loss of custody.

Supreme Court E-Library, Family Code ↗
2. Civil Code (Republic Act No. 386) — human relations, damages and civil liability

Current framework: Civil Code Articles 19, 20 and 21 continue to provide standards concerning the exercise of rights and performance of duties, unlawful acts causing damage, and willful injury contrary to morals, good customs or public policy. Article 26 may also be relevant to specified violations of dignity, personality, privacy and peace of mind. These provisions may support a civil claim where the required elements and proof exist; they do not automatically establish criminal liability or damages merely because conduct is considered unfair or hurtful.

Supreme Court E-Library, Civil Code ↗
3. Revised Penal Code, as amended — theft, malicious mischief, physical injuries, threats and defamation

Current framework: The Revised Penal Code remains in force, but later statutes have amended particular provisions and penalties. Republic Act No. 10951 (2017) adjusted monetary thresholds, values and fines under numerous RPC provisions, including provisions relevant to physical injuries and property offenses. Articles 308 and 327 remain relevant to theft and malicious mischief; Articles 262–266 address physical injuries and maltreatment; Articles 282 and 285 address specified threats; and Articles 353–359 address libel and oral defamation. The exact amended text, elements, penalties, prescription rules and any special-law overlap must be checked for the date and facts of a specific incident.

RA 10951, Senate Bureau ↗ RPC, SC E-Library ↗
4. Republic Act No. 9262 — Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act of 2004

Current framework: RA 9262 provides protection and remedies for covered acts of physical, sexual, psychological and economic abuse against a woman who is or was a spouse, dating or sexual partner, or has a common child with the offender, and against her child as defined by the Act. Psychological violence may include conduct such as repeated verbal abuse, harassment, public humiliation, or causing a victim to witness abuse, when the statutory elements are met. The Act has relationship and victim requirements; it is not a general criminal statute for every family disagreement or every allegation involving a child. It was enacted in 2004 and does not govern criminal liability for conduct in 1990–1994.

Philippine Commission on Women, RA 9262 ↗
5. Republic Act No. 11313 (2019) — Safe Spaces Act

Current framework: The Safe Spaces Act addresses gender-based sexual harassment in public spaces, online spaces, workplaces and educational or training institutions, as defined in the statute. It may be relevant to qualifying gender-based sexual harassment, including certain online conduct, but it should not be treated as a general law for all defamatory or emotionally harmful statements.

Philippine Commission on Women, Laws and Policies ↗
6. Republic Act No. 9255 — illegitimate child’s use of father’s surname

Current framework: RA 9255 amended Family Code Article 176 to allow an illegitimate child to use the father’s surname when the required express recognition and other statutory conditions are satisfied. The law includes a retroactivity provision subject to protection of vested or acquired rights. This amendment took effect long after 1990–1994 and should be distinguished from the original Article 176 applicable to the historical period.

Lawphil Official Text, RA 9255 ↗
7. Succession and legitimes — Civil Code and Family Code provisions, subject to current interpretation

Current framework: The applicable rules on compulsory heirs, legitimes, surviving spouses, legitimate and illegitimate children, and estate distribution depend on the law governing the estate at the relevant death and the person’s established civil status and filiation. Civil Code provisions remain important, but Article 992 and related succession questions have been subject to constitutional and jurisprudential interpretation. A historical wording should not be presented as an unqualified statement of current law without checking controlling decisions and the exact family facts.

Civil Code, Supreme Court E-Library ↗
8. Current lawyer ethics — Code of Professional Responsibility and Accountability (A.M. No. 22-09-01-SC, 2023)

Current framework: The 2023 Code of Professional Responsibility and Accountability (CPRA) is the current professional-conduct framework and replaced the 1988 Code of Professional Responsibility for current disciplinary matters, subject to the CPRA’s transition and procedural rules. It governs lawyers’ ethical duties and disciplinary accountability. The former Rule 1.01 and Rule 7.03 may remain relevant when describing the standards in force during 1990–1994, but current conduct should be analyzed under the CPRA rather than treating the 1988 Code as the present code.

Supreme Court, CPRA (A.M. No. 22-09-01-SC) ↗
9. Republic Act No. 10175 — Cybercrime Prevention Act (where online conduct is involved)

Current framework: RA 10175 may be relevant to qualifying offenses committed through information and communications technologies, including online libel under the statutory framework. The underlying offense and the law’s jurisdictional and constitutional limits must be considered. It does not automatically convert every online accusation, message or dispute into a cybercrime.

Remembering Atty. Byron Viray Belarmino

Beyond his profession, Atty. Byron Viray Belarmino was a man defined by empathy, kindness, patience, and quiet strength. Though wonderfully human and not without imperfections, as all people are, he carried a deep and unwavering regard for the innate dignity of others. He viewed every person as an equal, looking beyond distinctions of wealth, status, appearance, or circumstance. He believed that every individual deserved fairness, respect, and humane treatment—values he held close to his heart, even when his generosity and open-hearted kindness were taken advantage of by others.

Above all, he was a loving and deeply devoted father who gave everything he could to bring happiness, comfort, and opportunity to his children. He provided generously within his means, striving to give them the very best he could afford, while pouring his time, energy, and whole heart into their lives. His love was expressed both through what he provided and through the thoughtful, personal ways he showed his care, including his beloved hobby of handcrafting toys for his children. These creations were more than simple playthings; they were expressions of a father's affection, creativity, and desire to bring joy to those he loved.

For Atty. Belarmino, the practice of law was never solely about financial gain. He frequently accepted cases for modest compensation, particularly for clients who struggled to afford legal services, and extended his counsel wherever help was truly needed. Quiet by nature, warm and gentle in his demeanour, he valued substance over appearances, sincerity over pretence, and compassion over self-importance. He never measured a person's worth by wealth or social standing, but by the quality of their humanity. His kindness was reflected in his willingness to listen, to help, to give generously of himself, and to treat others with patience, consideration, and grace—quite the opposite of what narcissistic families or Machiavellian individuals value, where intelligence, wealth, beauty, status, attention, and personal usefulness are too often mistaken for measures of human worth.

His pursuit of truth and reverence for the law were inseparable from these personal values. To him, fairness was more than a legal principle; it was a responsibility reflected in how people were seen, heard, respected, and treated. He believed in the importance of truth, the dignity of every individual, and the equal application of justice, regardless of a person's circumstances or standing.

He was not without flaws, but in the things and people he held dear, his love was sincere and free of malice or selfishness. He gave what he could, helped where he was able, and treated those around him with a kindness that did not depend on recognition or reward. His legacy lives not only in the laws he practised and the cases recorded in his name, but in the compassion he showed, the generosity he extended, the patience he offered, and the profound love he gave to his children.

May the truth of his life endure, preserved in its entirety and never distorted by time, circumstance, or the interpretations of others. May he be remembered with honesty and with the compassion due to every human life—not as a man without flaws, but as the man he truly was, in all his sincerity, kindness, convictions, and love. May his memory remain untainted, his truth never rewritten, and the legacy of his life speak for itself, long after the pages of the law have faded.
