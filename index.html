I. Core laws and provisions around 1990–1994

1. Family Code (Executive Order No. 209) — marital duties and relief
Law and period: Effective August 3, 1988. Articles 68, 69 and 72 are relevant.

Rule / direct statutory text (Article 68): “The husband and wife are obliged to live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity, and render mutual help and support.”

Rule / statutory summary (Article 72): Article 72 permits an aggrieved spouse to apply to the court for relief when the other spouse neglects duties to the conjugal union or commits acts tending to bring danger, dishonor, or injury to the other spouse or the family.

1A. Family Code Articles 105–106 — conjugal partnership of gains
Law and period: Executive Order No. 209, Family Code, effective August 3, 1988; subject to its transitional provisions and any applicable marriage settlement.

Rule / statutory summary: Article 75 provides that, in the absence of a marriage settlement or where the settlement is void, the property relations of spouses are governed by the absolute community of property regime, subject to the Family Code’s transitional provisions. Article 105 governs when the conjugal partnership of gains regime applies, including specified partnerships established before the Family Code took effect, without prejudice to vested rights. Article 106 describes the conjugal partnership as the spouses’ common fund, into which the proceeds, products, fruits and income specified by law, and property acquired by either or both spouses through their work or industry or by chance, are placed, subject to statutory exclusions and the applicable property regime. Equal division upon dissolution and liquidation is addressed by the applicable liquidation provisions, including Article 129(7) for the conjugal partnership of gains, rather than by Article 106 alone.

Application and limit: The applicable regime depends on the date of marriage, any valid marriage settlement, transitional rules, and the classification and source of each asset. The existence of a conjugal partnership does not mean that every asset is automatically conjugal or that each spouse owns an immediate one-half physical share of every individual property.

2. Family Code — annulment and legal separation
Law and period: Articles 36, 45–47, 55–56, 58, 63–64.

Rule / statutory summary: Article 45 lists grounds for annulment of a voidable marriage. Article 36 addresses psychological incapacity existing at the time of marriage. Article 69 addresses the fixing of the family domicile and provides a mechanism for resolving disagreement between spouses, subject to the statutory qualification. Article 55(8) identifies sexual infidelity or perversion as a ground for legal separation; Article 55(10) addresses abandonment without justifiable cause for more than one year. Article 56 lists grounds for denial of legal separation.

IMPORTANT CLARIFICATION: DEATH BEFORE FINALIZATION OF AN ANNULMENT

Rule applicable: If a spouse died while an annulment case was pending and before a final judgment annulling the marriage, the pending case did not itself annul the marriage. Death ended the marital relationship by operation of law. The court could not thereafter issue a final annulment decree in the ordinary pending case; the personal marital action generally became moot or abated/was terminated.

What that means for a voidable marriage: An annulment under Family Code Articles 45–47 concerns a voidable marriage: it is treated as valid unless and until annulled by a final judgment. Therefore, if the spouse died before such a judgment became final, the marriage had not been annulled during the spouses’ lifetimes. Accordingly, the parties should not be described as legally annulled before the death unless the court record establishes that a final judgment had already taken effect.

Annulment should be distinguished from a declaration of nullity: A declaration of nullity concerns a marriage alleged to have been void from the beginning, including grounds under Articles 35–38 and Article 36. That is different from annulment of a voidable marriage. Death ends the union in either situation, but whether the marriage was void from inception may remain relevant to estate and succession disputes. Filing a petition alone does not establish that the marriage was void or that a decree was granted.

Historical authority and later procedural rule: In Lapuz v. Eufemio, G.R. No. L-30977 (January 31, 1972), the Supreme Court explained that a pending nullity action became moot upon a spouse’s death and that an annulment action involving a voidable marriage was extinguished upon death under the then-applicable Civil Code. Property questions could be resolved in proper estate or partition proceedings. The primary holding in Lapuz concerned the abatement of a pending legal-separation action upon the wife’s death; the Court’s discussion of nullity and voidable-marriage principles was secondary reasoning that reinforced the rule that the personal marital action did not proceed after death. Articles 63 and 64 specify effects of a decree of legal separation, including consequences for succession and property relations. Where death occurs before entry of a final decree, those decree-dependent effects do not arise merely from the filing or pendency of the action. Later, the 2003 Rule on Declaration of Absolute Nullity and Annulment of Voidable Marriages expressly provided that if a party dies before entry of judgment, the court shall close and terminate the case, without prejudice to estate settlement in proper proceedings. That 2003 rule was not in force in 1994 and is a later procedural clarification, not the rule then governing.

3. Family Code — custody and parental authority
Law and period: Articles 209–215, especially 211–214.

Rule / direct statutory text (Article 213): “No child under seven years of age shall be separated from the mother, unless the court finds compelling reasons to order otherwise.”

Rule / statutory summary: Article 211 provides for joint parental authority over common children and, in the original text applicable during 1990–1994, stated: “In case of disagreement, the father’s decision shall prevail, unless there is a judicial order to the contrary.” That period-specific wording is recorded as historical text and should not be assumed to state the controlling rule for current disputes without considering later law and jurisprudence. Article 212 governs the continuation and exercise of parental authority in situations contemplated by the Family Code when one parent cannot exercise such authority. Article 213 governs parental separation and judicial custody determinations, including the statutory considerations applicable to children over seven years of age. Article 214 addresses substitute parental authority in specified cases.

4. Rules of Court — Rule 99, Section 6 (historical custody procedure)
Law and period: Rules of Court effective January 1, 1964; Rule 99, Section 6 addressed proceedings concerning children whose parents were living separately or whose custody was in dispute.

Summary of historical rule: The historical rule directed the court to award care, custody, and control according to the child’s best interests; it included a child-preference rule for a child over ten, subject to unfitness, and a rule against separating a child under seven from the mother absent compelling reasons.

5. Family Code — support obligations
Law and period: Articles 194–200, particularly 194–196 and 199–200.

Rule / statutory summary: Article 194 defines support to include everything indispensable for sustenance, dwelling, clothing, medical attendance, education and transportation, in keeping with family resources. Article 195 identifies persons obliged to support one another, including spouses and parents and their legitimate and illegitimate children. Articles 199–200 set the order and proportional allocation among support obligors, with provisional support possible in urgent circumstances.

8. Revised Penal Code — concubinage
Law and period: Act No. 3815, Article 334; in force during 1990–1994.

Rule / statutory summary: Article 334 penalizes a husband who (1) keeps a mistress in the conjugal dwelling; (2) has sexual intercourse under scandalous circumstances with a woman who is not his wife; or (3) cohabits with her in another place. The provision separately states the penalty applicable to the concubine.

PENALTY STATES AND PROCEDURAL REQUIREMENTS APPLICABLE TO THE CONCUBINE

Statutory penalty (destierro): Article 334 states the penalty applicable to the concubine, while Article 87 sets the rules governing destierro. For the exact penalty and its legal conditions, consult the linked statutory text.

Evasion of service of sentence (Article 157, RPC): In People v. Abilong, G.R. No. L-1960 (Nov. 26, 1948), the Supreme Court affirmed the sentence of two years, four months and one day of prision correccional. The decision treated entry into the prohibited area during destierro as evasion of service of sentence under Article 157, whose statutory penalty range was prision correccional in its medium and maximum periods. This summarizes the majority decision; the case also contains a dissent.

Procedural requirements (Article 344, RPC and applicable criminal procedure): Concubinage may be prosecuted only upon the complaint required by Article 344 from the offended spouse. Where applicable criminal procedure requires both alleged offenders to be included and both are alive, that requirement governs. The legal effect of any alleged pardon, consent, or condonation depends on the relevant facts and governing law.

9. Lawyer discipline — Rule 138 §27 and 1988 Code of Professional Responsibility
Law and period: Rule 138 §27 of the Rules of Court; 1988 Code of Professional Responsibility, Rules 1.01 and 7.03 (promulgated June 21, 1988).

Rule / statutory summary: Rule 138 §27 permits suspension or removal on grounds listed in the rule, including grossly immoral conduct, malpractice or gross misconduct, conviction of a crime involving moral turpitude, and violation of the lawyer’s oath.

Rule / statutory summary (Rule 7.03): Rule 7.03 addresses conduct adversely reflecting on fitness to practice and scandalous behavior discrediting the profession.

11. Civil Code — succession and legitimes
Law and period: Civil Code Articles 777, 887 and 895 and applicable succession provisions, including Articles 992 and 996, subject to the law governing the estate at death.

Rule / statutory summary: Article 777 provides that hereditary rights are transmitted from the moment of death. Articles 887 and 895 address compulsory heirs and legitimes. Article 992 governs certain succession relationships between legitimate and illegitimate relatives and must be read together with controlling jurisprudence. Article 996 governs the concurrence of a surviving spouse with legitimate children or descendants in intestate succession. Their application depends on established filiation, civil status, the facts of the estate, and controlling law.

11A. Civil Code Articles 739 and 1028 — specified donations and testamentary dispositions
Law and period: Republic Act No. 386, Civil Code; provisions in force during 1990–1994, subject to later controlling interpretation.

Rule / statutory summary: Article 739 identifies categories of donations that are void, including donations between persons guilty of adultery or concubinage at the time of donation, donations between persons found guilty of the same criminal offense in consideration thereof, and donations to specified public officers or their relatives by reason of office. Article 1028 applies the prohibitions in Article 739 concerning donations inter vivos to testamentary provisions. Article 739 provides that the spouse of the donor or donee may bring an action for a declaration of nullity of the donation; the guilt of the donor and donee may be proved by a preponderance of evidence in that action, subject to the statutory text.

Application and limit: The provision applies where its statutory conditions are established for the donation or testamentary disposition at issue. It does not automatically invalidate every transfer involving an unmarried partner or every transaction between persons whose relationship is disputed.

Civil Code, Supreme Court E-Library ↗
11B. Civil Code Articles 428–429 — ownership and protection of property
Law and period: Republic Act No. 386, Civil Code; in force during 1990–1994.

Rule / statutory summary: Article 428 describes the owner’s rights to enjoy and dispose of property, subject to established limitations, and to bring an action against the holder or possessor to recover it. Article 429 permits an owner or lawful possessor to use reasonably necessary force to repel or prevent an actual or threatened unlawful physical invasion or usurpation of property, within the limits of the law.

Application and limit: Application depends on proof of the relevant ownership or possessory interest and the circumstances of the alleged interference. These provisions do not by themselves establish title, authorize disproportionate force, or resolve a disputed inheritance or marital-property classification.

14A. Civil Code Article 723 — private communications in writing
Law and period: Republic Act No. 386, Article 723; in force during 1990–1994.

Rule / direct statutory text: “Letters and other private communications in writing are owned by the person to whom they are addressed and delivered, but they cannot be published or disseminated without the consent of the writer or his heirs. However, the court may authorize their publication or dissemination if the public good or the interest of justice so requires.”

Application and limit: The provision concerns letters and other private communications in writing that fall within its terms. Ownership, delivery, consent, the nature of the communication, and the circumstances of any publication or dissemination matter. It is not an automatic finding of liability for every disclosure, and the statutory court-authorisation exception must be preserved.

16. Revised Penal Code Article 282 — grave threats
Law and period: Article 282, as in force on the date of the conduct under consideration.

Rule / statutory summary: Addresses a threat to inflict on another person, honor or property, or that of their family, a wrong amounting to a crime, with statutory distinctions including whether a condition is imposed.

Application: Potentially relevant to a communicated threat to kill or commit another crime. The legal characterization depends on the precise words communicated, context, intent, and surrounding conduct; an expression wishing death on a person is not, without more, necessarily a threat by the speaker to kill that person.

18A. Rules of Court, Rule 130, Sections 3–6 — best-evidence/original-document rule and secondary evidence
Law and period: For proceedings during 1990–1994, the applicable reference is the 1989 Revised Rules on Evidence, effective July 1, 1989, as identified by the Supreme Court E-Library. The rules were later amended effective May 1, 2020.

Historical terminology (1990–1994): During the period under examination, Rule 130, Section 3 was formally known as the Best Evidence Rule. The 2019 amendments to the Rules on Evidence, effective May 1, 2020, renamed it the Original Document Rule.

Rule / evidentiary summary: Under the historical rules, the Best Evidence Rule addressed proof of document contents when those contents were in issue, while the related provisions governed when secondary evidence could be received, including specified situations involving an unavailable original, an original in the adverse party’s custody or control, or voluminous records. The exact section numbers, required foundation, notice, and exceptions depend on the version applicable to the proceeding. The 2019 amendments effective May 1, 2020 use the term Original Document Rule and changed aspects of the evidence rules.

Application and limit: A copy, printout, transcription, or summary is not automatically admissible merely because it reproduces purported contents. Admissibility depends on the applicable rule, the reason the original is not produced, the required foundation, and any objection or exception. This rule alone does not prove alteration, falsification, authorship, or intent.

1989 Revised Rules on Evidence, Supreme Court E-Library ↗ Rules on Evidence, Lawphil ↗
18B. Rules of Court, Rule 132, Sections 20–21 — proof of private documents
Law and period: For proceedings during 1990–1994, the applicable reference is the 1989 Revised Rules on Evidence, effective July 1, 1989, as identified by the Supreme Court E-Library. Rule 132’s evidence provisions were later amended effective May 1, 2020.

Rule / evidentiary summary: Rule 132 Sections 20–21 in the historical rules concern proof of the due execution and authenticity of private documents, including testimony from a person who saw the document executed or written, or evidence of the genuineness of the maker’s signature or handwriting, subject to the rule’s provisions and exceptions. The applicable version and authentication method depend on the rules governing the proceeding.

Application and limit: Authentication addresses whether a document is what its proponent claims it to be; it does not automatically establish that every assertion within the document is true. Where disputed, consider authorship, due execution, completeness, alterations, provenance, and the method used to create and preserve a copy. These are evidentiary questions, not findings that any particular document was falsified.

1989 Revised Rules on Evidence, Supreme Court E-Library ↗ Rules on Evidence, Lawphil ↗
18C. Civil Code Article 4 and Constitution Article III, Section 22 — non-retroactivity and ex post facto laws
Law and period: Civil Code Article 4 states the general rule against retroactive effect unless otherwise provided; the Constitution prohibits ex post facto laws and bills of attainder.

Rule / legal principle: Article 4 provides that laws shall have no retroactive effect unless the contrary is provided. Article III, Section 22 of the 1987 Constitution prohibits ex post facto laws and bills of attainder. The constitutional prohibition is directed to penal legislation of the prohibited kind; it does not establish a blanket rule invalidating every later procedural or evidentiary amendment in a pending case.

Application and limit: For events said to have occurred in 1990–1994, distinguish the substantive law in force on the date of the alleged conduct from later amendments and from procedural rules governing a later proceeding. Retroactivity is determined by the particular law, its effectivity and transitional provisions, and controlling jurisprudence.

23A. Civil Code Articles 2219, 2229 and 2232–2234 — moral and exemplary damages
Law and period: Republic Act No. 386, Civil Code, as applied with later amendments and controlling jurisprudence.

Rule / statutory summary: Article 2219 identifies specified cases in which moral damages may be recovered, including listed categories of quasi-delicts and criminal offenses, subject to the article’s terms. Article 2229 describes exemplary or corrective damages as imposed by way of example or correction for the public good. Articles 2232–2234 set conditions for exemplary damages in specified civil cases, including circumstances involving wanton, fraudulent, reckless or malevolent conduct and other statutory requirements.

Application and limit: Moral or exemplary damages are not automatic. The claimant must establish a recognised legal basis, the applicable elements, and the evidence required by the governing provision and jurisprudence. The fact that conduct is hurtful or disputed does not alone establish entitlement to either category of damages.

III. CURRENT PHILIPPINE LAWS AND LEGAL UPDATES AS OF 2026
1. Family Code (Executive Order No. 209, as amended) — marriage, parental authority, custody and support

Current framework: The Family Code remains a principal source for marital obligations, legal separation and annulment, parental authority, custody, filiation, and family support. Articles 68–72 address marital duties and relief; Articles 194–203 address support; Articles 209–230 address parental authority and responsibilities. Article 213 addresses child custody when parents are separated and retains the rule that a child under seven should not be separated from the mother absent compelling reasons, while the child’s best interests and parental fitness remain central to custody decisions. Application depends on the facts, the applicable statutory text, and current Supreme Court interpretations; an allegation alone does not establish a ground for annulment, legal separation, or loss of custody.

Supreme Court E-Library, Family Code ↗
2. Civil Code (Republic Act No. 386) — human relations, damages and civil liability

Current framework: Civil Code Articles 19, 20 and 21 continue to provide standards concerning the exercise of rights and performance of duties, unlawful acts causing damage, and willful injury contrary to morals, good customs or public policy. Article 26 may also be relevant to specified violations of dignity, personality, privacy and peace of mind. These provisions may support a civil claim where the required elements and proof exist; they do not automatically establish criminal liability or damages merely because conduct is considered unfair or hurtful.

Supreme Court E-Library, Civil Code ↗
3. Revised Penal Code, as amended — theft, malicious mischief, physical injuries, threats and defamation

Current framework: The Revised Penal Code remains in force, but later statutes have amended particular provisions and penalties. Republic Act No. 10951 (2017) adjusted monetary thresholds, values and fines under numerous RPC provisions, including provisions relevant to physical injuries and property offenses. Articles 308 and 327 remain relevant to theft and malicious mischief; Articles 262–266 address physical injuries and maltreatment; Articles 282 and 285 address specified threats; and Articles 353–359 address libel and oral defamation. The exact amended text, elements, penalties, prescription rules and any special-law overlap depend on the date and facts of the incident.

RA 10951, Senate Bureau ↗ RPC, SC E-Library ↗
4. Republic Act No. 9262 — Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act of 2004

Current framework: RA 9262 provides protection and remedies for covered acts of physical, sexual, psychological and economic abuse against a woman who is or was a spouse, dating or sexual partner, or has a common child with the offender, and against her child as defined by the Act. Psychological violence may include conduct such as repeated verbal abuse, harassment, public humiliation, or causing a victim to witness abuse, when the statutory elements are met. The Act has relationship and victim requirements; it is not a general criminal statute for every family disagreement or every allegation involving a child. It was enacted in 2004 and does not govern criminal liability for conduct in 1990–1994.

RA 9262, Lawphil statutory text ↗
5. Republic Act No. 11313 (2019) — Safe Spaces Act

Current framework: The Safe Spaces Act addresses gender-based sexual harassment in public spaces, online spaces, workplaces and educational or training institutions, as defined in the statute. It may be relevant to qualifying gender-based sexual harassment, including certain online conduct, but it is not a general law governing all defamatory or emotionally harmful statements.

RA 11313, Lawphil statutory text ↗
6. Republic Act No. 9255 — illegitimate child’s use of father’s surname

Current framework: RA 9255 amended Family Code Article 176 to allow an illegitimate child to use the father’s surname when the required express recognition and other statutory conditions are satisfied. The law includes a retroactivity provision subject to protection of vested or acquired rights. This amendment took effect long after 1990–1994 and should be distinguished from the original Article 176 applicable to the historical period.

7. Succession and legitimes — Civil Code and Family Code provisions, subject to current interpretation

Current framework: The applicable rules on compulsory heirs, legitimes, surviving spouses, legitimate and illegitimate children, and estate distribution depend on the law governing the estate at the relevant death and the person’s established civil status and filiation. Civil Code provisions remain important, but Article 992 and related succession questions have been subject to constitutional and jurisprudential interpretation. Historical statutory wording must be read alongside controlling decisions and the relevant facts before it is stated as current law.

8. Rules on Electronic Evidence, Rule 5 §§1–2 — authentication of electronic documents
Law and period: A.M. No. 01-7-01-SC, Rules on Electronic Evidence; promulgated in 2001 and effective August 1, 2001. This was not in force during 1990–1994.

Rule / statutory summary: Rule 5 §1 places the burden of proving authenticity on the person seeking to introduce an electronic document. Section 2 requires a private electronic document offered as authentic to be authenticated by evidence of a digital signature, appropriate security procedures or devices, or other evidence showing integrity and reliability to the satisfaction of the judge.

Application and limit: An electronic document is not automatically inadmissible merely because it is electronic, but admissibility and weight depend on the Rules of Court, applicable law, authentication, integrity, reliability, and the surrounding circumstances. Authentication does not automatically prove the truth of every statement in the document. The rule’s 2001 effective date is distinct from the 1990–1994 historical period.

9. Current lawyer ethics — Code of Professional Responsibility and Accountability (A.M. No. 22-09-01-SC, 2023)

Current framework: The 2023 Code of Professional Responsibility and Accountability (CPRA) is the current professional-conduct framework and replaced the 1988 Code of Professional Responsibility for current disciplinary matters, subject to the CPRA’s transition and procedural rules. It governs lawyers’ ethical duties and disciplinary accountability. The former Rule 1.01 and Rule 7.03 may remain relevant when describing the standards in force during 1990–1994, but current conduct should be analyzed under the CPRA rather than treating the 1988 Code as the present code.

10. Republic Act No. 10175 — Cybercrime Prevention Act (where online conduct is involved)

Current framework: RA 10175 may be relevant to qualifying offenses committed through information and communications technologies, including online libel under the statutory framework. Its application depends on the elements of the underlying offense and the statute’s jurisdictional and constitutional limits; not every online accusation, message, or dispute constitutes a cybercrime.

Remembering Atty. Byron Viray Belarmino

Beyond his profession, Atty. Byron Viray Belarmino was a man defined by empathy, kindness, patience, and quiet strength. Though wonderfully human and not without imperfections, as all people are, he carried a deep and unwavering regard for the innate dignity of others. He viewed every person as an equal, looking beyond distinctions of wealth, status, appearance, or circumstance. He believed that every individual deserved fairness, respect, and humane treatment—values he held close to his heart, even when his generosity and open-hearted kindness were taken advantage of by others.

Above all, he was a loving and deeply devoted father who gave everything he could to bring happiness, comfort, and opportunity to his children. He provided generously within his means, striving to give them the very best he could afford, while pouring his time, energy, and whole heart into their lives. His love was expressed both through what he provided and through the thoughtful, personal ways he showed his care, including his beloved hobby of handcrafting toys for his children. These creations were more than simple playthings; they were expressions of a father's affection, creativity, and desire to bring joy to those he loved.

For Atty. Belarmino, the practice of law was never solely about financial gain. He frequently accepted cases for modest compensation, particularly for clients who struggled to afford legal services, and extended his counsel wherever help was truly needed. Quiet by nature, warm and gentle in his demeanour, he valued substance over appearances, sincerity over pretence, and compassion over self-importance. He never measured a person's worth by wealth or social standing, but by the quality of their humanity. His kindness was reflected in his willingness to listen, to help, to give generously of himself, and to treat others with patience, consideration, and grace.

His pursuit of truth and reverence for the law were inseparable from these personal values. To him, fairness was more than a legal principle; it was a responsibility reflected in how people were seen, heard, respected, and treated. He believed in the importance of truth, the dignity of every individual, and the equal application of justice, regardless of a person's circumstances or standing.

He was not without flaws, but in the things and people he held dear, his love was sincere and free of malice or selfishness. He gave what he could, helped where he was able, and treated those around him with a kindness that did not depend on recognition or reward. His legacy lives not only in the laws he practised and the cases recorded in his name, but in the compassion he showed, the generosity he extended, the patience he offered, and the profound love he gave to his children.

May the truth of his life endure, preserved in its entirety and never distorted by time, circumstance, or the interpretations of others. May he be remembered with honesty and with the compassion due to every human life—not as a man without flaws, but as the man he truly was, in all his sincerity, kindness, convictions, and love. May his memory remain untainted, his truth never rewritten, and the legacy of his life speak for itself, long after the pages of the law have faded.
